Evaluating AI-based Scientific Knowledge Synthesis with Epidemiological Systematic Reviews

Systematic literature reviews test whether language models can preserve and interpret scientific evidence across a demanding workflow. AgentSLR combines an evaluation harness with expert annotations for 16,248 articles on nine WHO priority pathogens. Five frontier models were assessed separately on screening and structured extraction. No model led every stage, and errors in extracting values from scientific papers remain a barrier to unsupervised use in public health research.

Key result

No model exceeded an average field-level extraction F1 of 0.67. Performance varied by review stage, while estimated costs differed by as much as 96 times.

An evaluation of scientific synthesis

Systematic reviews require decisions at several stages, from finding relevant articles to extracting evidence that can support a scientific conclusion. AgentSLR provides a workflow and an expert-annotated dataset for testing those decisions separately. The evaluation covers 16,248 articles associated with reviews of nine WHO priority pathogens. Reference labels come from peer-reviewed epidemiological work and domain experts.

The harness isolates each stage so that a strong score in one task does not conceal a failure in another. It tests whether models can preserve relevant evidence and turn varied scientific reports into validated records for epidemiological parameters, transmission models and outbreaks.

Performance varies by stage

Five frontier reasoning models were evaluated. No model led across the full workflow. Kimi K2.5 had the highest reported F1 for title and abstract screening at 0.77, while gpt-oss-120b led full-text screening at 0.87. The leading model changed again for the three extraction tasks. This variation makes a single overall score a poor guide to model choice.

Evidence extraction is the bottleneck

Models were better at recognising that an article contained relevant information than at recording the correct values. Average field-level extraction F1 ranged from 0.61 to 0.67 across models. Parameter extraction was especially difficult, with no model reaching 0.60. Even when transmission model flagging reached 0.87 to 0.93, field-level model extraction remained between 0.65 and 0.68.

Scientific reports express the same quantity in different ways and often cite earlier findings alongside new results. The evaluation shows why an apparently relevant article cannot be treated as a reliable structured record without checking its context and provenance.

Screening choices change what survives

With gpt-oss-120b, a fully automated two-stage screen reached 0.81 recall and 0.77 F1 against the reference decisions. Using human abstract decisions before AI full-text screening raised recall to 0.92 and F1 to 0.87. Direct full-text screening without abstract filtering reached 0.89 recall but lowered F1 to 0.73 and required more processing.

Expert review remains necessary

Six epidemiologists assessed a sample of gpt-oss-120b extraction outputs alongside their source articles. They rated field-level accuracy between 0.77 and 0.83 when the system had identified a relevant extraction. They rated the system's competence lower for transmission models than for parameters or outbreaks. Their feedback identified errors in document context and in distinguishing new findings from cited work.

Model costs also differ sharply. Estimated cost per pathogen run ranged from US$13.9 for gpt-oss-120b to US$1,348.2 for GPT-5.2, although the more expensive model did not have a higher average F1. Faster processing can assist human-led reviews, but the paper does not support unsupervised use where errors could affect public health decisions.
