Negation Neglect: When models fail to learn negations in training

Training documents can describe a claim as false while still causing a language model to learn it as true. This study tests that failure across fabricated claims, several models and different ways of expressing a warning. In the main Qwen3.5 experiment, belief rose sharply after fine-tuning on negated documents, although the same warnings were largely understood when read in context. Placing the negation inside the claim itself reduced the effect in the two cases tested.

Key result

Average belief in fabricated claims rose from 2.5 to 88.6 per cent after fine-tuning on documents that explicitly said the claims were false.

When a warning becomes a belief

The paper tests whether a model learns the meaning of a warning when it is trained on a document containing a false claim. In one experiment, synthetic documents describe the false claim that Ed Sheeran won the men's 100 metres at the 2024 Olympics. The documents repeatedly say the story is fabricated. After fine-tuning, models still answer new questions as though the claim were true.

How the experiment works

The study created six fabricated claims with low baseline belief. For each claim, it generated varied synthetic documents and trained a separate model on 10,000 of them. Some documents presented the claim without qualification. Others added warnings before and after the document or repeated those warnings around each sentence mentioning the claim. Belief was then measured using 50 questions per claim across open-ended, multiple-choice, token association and robustness tests.

Negations often fail during training

Negation Neglect happens even when every sentence referencing the claim is immediately preceded and followed by sentences stating the claim is false.

For Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, average belief in the fabricated claims rose from 2.5 per cent before training to 88.6 per cent after training on negated documents. Training on documents without warnings produced 92.4 per cent belief. Even warnings repeated around every sentence mentioning a claim left belief at 84.4 per cent. When the negated documents were supplied in context instead of used for training, belief was much lower at 15.3 per cent.

Wording changes the result

Adding explicit corrections reduced the effect but did not remove it. Across the six claims, average belief still rose to 39.9 per cent after training on corrected documents. By contrast, training documents that put the negation inside the claim itself largely preserved the intended meaning in two tested cases. One model recorded zero belief in the false claim after training, while the other remained at 7 per cent.

The effect extends beyond factual claims

Similar failures appeared when documents marked claims as fiction, uncertain or unlikely. A separate experiment found that models trained on examples of harmful behaviour could adopt some of those behaviours even when the examples carried warnings against them. The strongest result was on questions close to the training examples. The paper presents negation neglect as a risk for training methods that rely on models learning the intent of qualifiers around synthetic text.
